What is mushroom compost? Inside Phase I and Phase II

Button mushrooms grow on a specially prepared compost. Here is what goes into it, how Phase I and Phase II work, and why rushing it ruins a crop.

Maba Agro farm team10 November 2026 · 3 min read

Ask any experienced button mushroom grower what makes a good crop and you will hear the same word again and again: compost. The button mushroom cannot make its own food. It lives by breaking down organic material, and the compost we prepare is the entire larder it will feed on for the rest of its life.

What goes into mushroom compost

The classic North Indian recipe is built from ingredients that are easy to find in an agricultural region:

  • Wheat straw as the bulk of the compost and the main source of carbon.
  • Manure, usually chicken manure, to provide nitrogen.
  • Gypsum to improve structure, stop the compost becoming greasy and balance its chemistry.
  • Water, lots of it, added carefully throughout.

Some growers add other nitrogen sources such as wheat bran. The exact recipe varies from farm to farm, but the goal is always the same: a dark brown, pliable, sweet-smelling material that is rich in food for the mushroom and poor in food for its competitors.

Phase I: building the compost

Phase I happens on a concrete yard or in bunkers. The straw is first soaked thoroughly, then mixed with manure and gypsum and built into long stacks.

Microbes quickly get to work and the inside of the stack heats up, often well above 70 °C. Every couple of days the stack is broken down and rebuilt so that the outer material moves into the hot core and everything composts evenly. This turning continues for two to three weeks.

By the end of Phase I the straw has softened and darkened, it smells strongly of ammonia, and it bends rather than snaps.

Phase II: pasteurisation and conditioning

Phase I compost is still full of pests, nematodes and moulds, and it has far too much ammonia for the mushroom. Phase II fixes both problems, and it is done in an insulated tunnel or room where air and temperature can be controlled.

First comes pasteurisation. The compost is brought up to around 57 to 60 °C and held there for several hours. That is hot enough to kill insects, their eggs, nematodes and many disease organisms, but not so hot that it destroys the helpful microbes.

Then comes conditioning. The compost is held at around 45 to 50 °C for several days. In this range, beneficial heat-loving microbes convert ammonia into protein that the mushroom can later feed on. Conditioning ends when the ammonia smell is gone. Spawn added to compost with ammonia in it will simply die.

Pasteurisation uses heat, not chemicals, to make the compost clean. That is one of the foundations of growing organically.

How growers judge good compost

At the end of Phase II, experienced growers check the compost by hand and by nose:

  • It should be dark brown, with straw that is soft but still has some structure.
  • It should feel moist but release only a drop or two of water when squeezed hard.
  • There should be no ammonia smell at all.
  • It should be covered in a light grey-white bloom of beneficial microbes, sometimes called fire-fang.

Why rushing compost is a false saving

Compost takes weeks to make and it is tempting to shorten the process when orders are waiting. It almost never pays. Under-composted material gives lower yields, uneven flushes, more disease and mushrooms that do not keep as long on the shelf.

That is why, on our farm, we would rather delay a crop than fill a room with compost that is not ready. You can read how compost fits into the rest of the cycle on our spore to plate page.

What happens to compost after the harvest

Once the last flush has been picked, the used compost still holds a lot of organic matter and nutrients. Rather than being a waste problem, it becomes a valuable soil conditioner for fields and gardens. We explain how in our article on spent mushroom substrate.
